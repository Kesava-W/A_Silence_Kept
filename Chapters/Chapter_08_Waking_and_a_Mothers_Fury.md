# Chapter Eight: Waking, and a Mother's Fury

Madhava came out of the coma on the ninth day, blinking against the ward's grey light, and the first thing he properly saw was Sukanya's face above his, worn thin with days of not sleeping in any bed but that chair.

"Hey." Her voice cracked on the single word, and she didn't try to hide it. "Hey, look at me. Do you know where you are?"

"Hospital." His own voice came out as barely more than air. "Your hospital."

"My hospital." She was already reaching past him for the call button, her other hand not letting go of his. "Don't move. Don't try to sit up. I need to get someone in here, and then I need you to stay exactly where you are while I shout at you for a while, is that understood?"

He managed something close to a smile, cracked and pale as it was, and that, more than the monitors or the doctor who came running a minute later, was what finally let her breathe.

The first night, she told herself it was only the one night - that she'd go home once his chart had a number on it she trusted. By the third, she'd stopped pretending she needed to be asked twice to sit beside him, and the nurses had stopped telling her visiting hours applied to her at all, one of them quietly bringing a second pillow for the chair without either of them ever discussing it. By the sixth, Chitra had taken to leaving a tiffin box on the nurses' station counter with Sukanya's name written on the lid in marker, food she ate standing up, her eyes on the monitor rather than her own hands. By the ninth, she knew the particular rhythm of his machines well enough to have caught the change in it half a second before the nurse did - and she hadn't left the hospital, in all that time, except to change her clothes.

Haritha had come once during those nine days, on a weekend home from her postgraduate course she hadn't told anyone she was taking, and had sat a long while in the doorway before she worked up the courage to come further in - not because she doubted she was welcome, but because the sight of her brother wired to machines had stopped her at the threshold the way it hadn't the first time, years ago, when the stakes had been entirely his own doing. Sukanya had looked up from the chair, recognised something of herself in the girl's stillness, and simply said, "He's stable. He's going to be fine," and Haritha had nodded and come the rest of the way in, and the two of them had sat either side of the bed for the better part of an hour without needing to say very much else, two women who'd each, in their own way, already decided this man was worth the waiting.

Two days after he woke.

It was Manikanta and Sirisha who finally came to see for themselves what their daughter had turned into.

They found her exactly where the hospital gossip had said they would - in the chair, beside his bed, her hand loosely over his on the blanket. Manikanta took it in with a father's quiet, careful eyes and said nothing at first. Sirisha did not have his patience.

"What is this, Sukanya?" Her voice climbed before the question had even finished, carrying enough that a passing nurse slowed her step in the corridor before thinking better of it. "You've stopped eating at your own table. Stopped sleeping in your own house. For a man who is - what, exactly, to you?"

"He's the man I love, amma."

"You've known him a matter of weeks!"

"I've known him since the night I bandaged his ribs, and I've known what I feel for him for rather longer than I've said it out loud, if that's what you're asking."

"Devuda, naa koothurki emaindi!" (God, what's happened to my daughter!) "That's not knowing a person, that's a fever. It passes." Sirisha's hands were moving now, the way they did when words alone weren't carrying enough of what she felt. "Your father and I have watched this family for years from a distance, same as everyone in Ryali - a plant, a business, a name people nod at in the market. That is not the same as knowing what kind of house you'd be marrying into, what kind of man really lives behind that name."

"Then get to know him, amma. Ask him anything you like. He's right here." Sukanya's voice hadn't risen to match her mother's, but there was an edge in it now that hadn't been there a moment ago. "Or is it that you've already decided, and you'd rather be angry at me than curious about him?"

That landed harder than Sukanya had meant it to, and for a moment Sirisha only stared at her, something in her face faltering before the anger found its footing again and came back stronger for the stumble.

"About outsiders," she said, the word coming out sharper than the last time she'd used it, "about a son we've exchanged sweets with and never once sat down to actually know. A daughter who studied medicine, who could have had her pick of good families, only to throw her sense away over a man half the village whispers strange things about." She said things in that flood she would likely wish, later, she hadn't said, the word "stranger" landing hardest of all - the same word Krishna had thrown out in jest weeks ago, come back now with all its teeth, aimed to wound rather than tease. "A stranger, Sukanya. That's what he is to this family, whatever he is to you."

"He is not a stranger." Sukanya's own voice cracked now, not from anger but from something rawer underneath it. "I have sat beside that bed for nine days, amma. I know exactly who I'm choosing. Do you?"

Manikanta let it run its course only so far. Then he took his wife gently by the arm and steered her, still talking, out of the ward and down the corridor, his voice pitched low beneath hers the whole way, the words themselves lost to Sukanya, who sat where she was, very still, one hand still over Madhava's, waiting to find out which parent would come back through that door.

It was her father who returned, alone, some while later, his face harder to read than it had been in years. He didn't sit. He stood at the foot of the bed and looked at his daughter for a long moment, and then at the man sleeping in it - really looked, the way he hadn't quite let himself in the doorway - and said only, "If this is what you want, Sukanya - then you have my blessing." A pause, longer than the sentence needed. "Your mother will come round to it. Give her time. She's not wrong to be frightened for you, even if she's wrong about the rest of it."

Sukanya's eyes filled, and she didn't trust herself to say anything at all beyond a small, cracked "Thank you, nanna," so she simply nodded, and reached again for Madhava's hand, and found, when she looked down, that his eyes had opened again while her father spoke, and had heard rather more of it than either of them had known.
