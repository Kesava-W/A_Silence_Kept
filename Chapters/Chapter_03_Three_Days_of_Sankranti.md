# Chapter Three: Three Days of Sankranti

Two days later, Ryali turned toward Sankranti.

It was the village's biggest festival, three days long, and by the first grey hour of Bhogi the courtyards up and down every lane had already been swept, washed with cow-dung and water until the packed earth shone, and laid over with rangoli in white rice-flour and colour - peacocks, lotuses, the sun in its many painted forms, competing for the eye from one gate to the next. Somewhere down the lane a Bhogi fire was already smoking, old wood and broken furniture and a child's outgrown chappals going up together the way they did every year, and the smell of it drifted over every courtyard regardless of whose fire it had actually been.

Sukanya was on her knees outside her own gate, working a border of dots into loops with the quick, practised hand of someone who'd been doing this since she was seven, her fingers already stained white to the second knuckle, when a bike slowed on the lane behind her.

She didn't need to look up to know whose engine that was. Half of Ryali would have known Madhava's bike by its cough on the second gear - on his way to check the plant despite the holiday, because a holiday for the village was not a holiday for an ethanol tank that didn't care what day it was, and Rajesh could only be trusted with so much before someone senior needed to look at the numbers himself.

He slowed anyway. Stopped, almost, without quite stopping, one foot down, looking at the half-finished rangoli the way a man looks at something he hadn't planned on admiring.

"That's a fine peacock," he said.

"It's not finished."

"It's fine anyway."

"You say that about everything unfinished. I've heard you say it about the second fermentation tank."

"The second fermentation tank is also fine." A small, dry twitch at the corner of his mouth, the closest thing to a grin he seemed to allow himself in public. "I stand by both opinions."

She looked up properly then, rice flour still caught on her fingers, and neither of them said anything further - a beat too long, the kind neither could have explained to anyone who asked - before he lifted his hand in something between a wave and a salute and rode on toward the plant, and she went back to her peacock with a small, private smile she didn't examine too closely, though her mother, sweeping the veranda behind her, examined it rather more than Sukanya would have liked.

That evening, the Bhogi fires long since burned down to embers, he came off the field road walking his bike rather than riding it, one hand pressed lightly to his side without seeming to notice he was doing it. Sukanya, back at her gate with the last of the rice flour still under her nails, saw it before he did.

"You rode to the plant today." Not a question. "On four ribs I taped myself, two days ago."

"They're better."

"They are bruised, Madhava, not healed, and I would know, since I'm the one who bandaged them." She crossed the lane without waiting to be asked whether she was welcome to. "You're not meant to be on a bike at all this week, let alone twice in one day."

"The plant doesn't observe Sankranti. Somebody has to look at the tanks."

"Rajesh can look at the tanks."

"Rajesh can read a gauge. He can't tell you why it's reading wrong." He said it plainly, the way he said most things, and it was somehow the plainness of it that annoyed her more than any argument would have.

"That's not an answer, that's a deflection, and I've heard enough of both this week to recognise the difference." Her voice had an edge in it now that surprised even her a little. "I didn't spend two nights worrying whether your lung would collapse so you could prove a point to a fermentation tank."

That stopped him. Whatever he'd been about to say next, he set it down instead and looked at her properly - really looked, the half-smile gone from his face for once. "Two nights."

"That's not the point."

"It rather sounds like the point." Something had shifted in his voice, quieter now, testing. "Sukanya -"

"Don't." She said it too fast, and heard herself say it too fast, and pressed on before he could notice the crack in it. "Don't turn this into - I'm your doctor. It's my professional opinion that you're an idiot, and I'll write it on the chart if I have to."

"You'd need me to come back in for that."

"Don't tempt me." But the anger in her voice had already thinned into something closer to the thing underneath it, and she pressed two fingers, harder than she meant to, into the exact spot on his side that was actually bruised.

He made a sound that was not quite a wince and not quite a laugh. "That," he said, "was not very doctor-like."

"That was entirely doctor-like. That's called a physical exam." But she was fighting a smile now, and losing, and he was watching her lose it with the satisfaction of a man who'd got exactly the reaction he'd been angling for the whole time. "Go home. Rest. Actually rest, Madhava - not the version of rest where you read ledgers in bed."

"I make no promises about the ledgers."

"Ayyo, nee ishtam!" (Fine, do as you like!) She threw up her hands, half-exasperated, half something else entirely, and he watched her go with a smile he still wasn't in the habit of showing anyone else, one hand pressed, more carefully now, back against his ribs.

Sankranti day itself arrived with new clothes on every child in the lane and the whole village smelling of ghee and jaggery from before sunrise, every kitchen given over to pongal - the sweet kind first, then the savoury, sacks of new rice opened for the year, a pot boiling over on purpose outside nearly every door for the luck of it, children shouting "Pongalo Pongal" at the exact moment the milk ran over and scalded itself against the clay. Families moved between each other's houses all morning in a loose, easy circuit of visiting that needed no formal invitation, only the season - a plate of sweets carried in, an older plate carried out half-emptied and refilled at the next house, the whole village's kitchens quietly trading places for a day.

Madhava's family and Sukanya's, known to each other the way most old Ryali families were, made their customary call sometime past noon - Prashanthi with a tray of sweets arranged with more care than the occasion strictly required, Vijay exchanging pleasantries with Manikanta about the year's paddy and whether the canal water would hold through February, Sirisha fussing over how thin Haritha had gotten at college and pressing a second ariselu on her before the first was finished. In the crowd of relatives filling the front room, somehow, without either of them arranging it, Sukanya and Madhava ended up seated across from each other, and said almost nothing, and looked rather more than either would have admitted, and both were faintly relieved when an aunt's loud opinion about the Kanuma cockfight - "Kodi pandelu ika maaneyyali, paapam kodipunjulu!" (These cockfights should stop already, poor roosters!) - gave them somewhere else to point their eyes. At one point Haritha, sitting beside her brother with a plate balanced on her knee, glanced between the two of them and then at nothing in particular, and kept whatever she'd noticed to herself, filing it away the way younger sisters do.

On Kanuma, the third day, the whole village turned out for Prabhala Theertham - the old chariot, carrying its image of Lord Shiva, drawn slowly through the fields and along the canal bunds and in under the coconut groves, drums and temple bells going on ahead of it, the crowd moving with it the way a tide moves, no one quite choosing to walk beside anyone in particular and yet somehow always ending up beside someone.

Sukanya and Madhava were swept into the same stretch of that crowd for the better part of an hour, close enough to talk over the drums and the temple bells, and talked of nothing that mattered.

"The chariot's slower than last year," she said.

"It's the same speed it's always been. You're just in more of a hurry than last year." He said it lightly, watching the road ahead rather than her, and she didn't answer that at all, only looked away toward the coconut groves as if the wheels there had suddenly become very interesting.

They talked of the heat instead, and the crowd, and whether the priest leading the procession had gotten slower with age or the crowd had simply gotten larger, and neither said the thing that sat, unspoken, in the space between their shoulders the whole slow walk through the fields: that something had started between them on a hospital bed, over a strip of gauze and a question about trust, and neither one, yet, was ready to give it a name. When the chariot finally reached the Theertham and the crowd surged forward around it, they were separated for a moment in the press of bodies, and each of them, briefly and without quite admitting it to themselves, spent that moment looking for the other before either was found again.
