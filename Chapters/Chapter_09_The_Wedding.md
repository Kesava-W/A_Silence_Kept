# Chapter Nine: The Wedding

Two months later, under a shamiana strung with mango leaves and marigold, in front of half of Ryali and the whole of both families, Madhava and Sukanya were married.

Sirisha, true to her husband's word, had come round to it by then - not loudly, not all at once, but slowly, over weeks of small unannounced visits Manikanta had quietly engineered, a cup of tea here, a question about the plant's expansion there, until the man behind the name had worn down enough of her worry that what remained of it was only a mother's ordinary fear rather than a stranger's suspicion. She was there at the muhurtham with her hands full of turmeric and rice, and she cried the way mothers cry at weddings regardless of how they'd felt about the groom two months earlier, and nobody thought it worth asking her which tears were which. At one point, catching Sukanya's eye across the ceremony fire, she managed a small, watery smile that was as close to an apology as either of them would ever put into words, and Sukanya, smiling back through her own tears, understood it exactly as it was meant.

Prashanthi wept openly and without shame through the whole ceremony, dabbing at her eyes with the end of her sari between mantras, pressing extra sweets into the hands of every relative who came near her as though joy needed to be fed to be believed, and telling anyone within earshot, more than once, "Sukhamga undandi!" (Be happy, always!) Vijay stood beside her, dry-eyed and rigid with the pride of fathers who don't have the words for it, though when the priest called for the couple to exchange garlands and the whole shamiana erupted into applause and whistling from Madhava's college friends, he was seen, briefly and by no one he'd have admitted it to, to wipe at one eye with the back of his hand before straightening again. Haritha, back from her postgraduate course for the occasion, sat close enough to her brother throughout the rituals that more than one aunt remarked on it, fondly, as though it were simply the closeness of siblings and nothing more, and when the ceremony reached the moment for the sister's blessing, her voice caught so badly on the verse that Prashanthi had to finish the line for her, squeezing her daughter's hand the whole while.

Krishna gave a small, teasing speech at the reception about strangers and stitches - "My sister met her husband because seven men decided to test how hard his ribs were, and somehow she's the one who ended up with a broken heart out of the whole business" - that made his sister throw a napkin at him across the table, and made Madhava laugh, properly laugh, for perhaps the first time anyone outside his own house had ever seen him do, a sound low and unguarded enough that Prashanthi, three tables away, turned at the sound of it as if she hadn't quite recognised her own son's laugh in years.

"I mean it, though," Krishna added, quieter now, raising his glass in Madhava's direction once the table had stopped laughing. "Take care of her. She doesn't ask for help easily. If she's asking you for anything at all, it's because it matters."

Madhava met his eyes across the table and nodded once, and something passed between the two men that needed no further words to close.

By the time the last guest had gone and the shamiana stood empty under the stars, waiting to be taken down in the morning, the two of them were, at last, simply married - no longer patient and doctor, no longer a village romance everyone had an opinion about, just a husband and a wife with a house to go home to.
