# Chapter One: The Night on the Field Road

The canal road out of Ryali ran narrow between the paddy and the dark - a raised spine of packed earth with water standing black on either side, just wide enough for a bullock cart to pass a bicycle if both riders knew to lean the right way. By day it carried bicycles and bullock carts and children cutting through to the coconut groves, their voices carrying further over standing water than they ever did on solid ground. By ten at night it carried no one but Madhava Raju, walking home from the plant, his shirt still smelling of molasses and steam, the day's noise - compressors, the hiss of a valve somebody hadn't quite closed right, Rajesh's voice reading out numbers off a clipboard - still settling out of his ears the way it always did on this stretch, half a kilometre at a time.

He had sent the bike back with Rajesh - the boy's own scooter had a flat, and the last bus to Atreyapuram didn't stop near his lane, and there was no sense in both of them losing sleep over one dead tyre. Madhava didn't mind the walk. He liked the field road at night, liked it in a way he'd never quite explained to anyone, not even Rajesh, who'd offered more than once to wait and ride him back on the pillion instead. The frogs going quiet as he passed, starting up again behind him, a wave of silence he carried with him and left in his wake. The Platanol chimney's low red warning light shrinking into the dark until it was just another star sitting too close to the ground. Three kilometres of that, and he'd be home, and his mother would have kept his plate covered on the stove whether he'd asked her to or not - she always did, on nights he worked late, as if the covering itself were a kind of prayer that he'd be back to lift it.

He was perhaps halfway there when the frogs went quiet and did not start again.

He noticed it the way he noticed a tank pressure drifting off spec - not alarm, just the cold attention of a man whose business had taught him to trust what changed, and to ask why before he asked what next. He slowed. The canal lay flat and silver under a moon three-quarters full, still enough to double the sky in it. The paddy on either side stood chest-high and rustling, though there was no wind he could feel on his own skin to account for it. Out of that rustling, taking their time, men began to come.

Six of them. Seven. Spreading from both sides of the road, closing the gaps before he'd finished counting, with the loose confidence of men who had done this before and expected it to go the same way it always had. Two carried knives loose at their sides, the blades catching thin lines of moon. The rest came empty-handed, which made them no less a wall - seven men on a raised earth road with black water on both sides leave a man exactly one direction to run, and every one of them knew it.

Madhava stopped. He turned once, slow, taking the shape of the ring around him - soft mud to his left, packed road under his own feet, the nearest gap in their circle already closing before he could have used it. He said nothing. There was nothing yet worth saying.

"Suribabu garu sends his regards," one of them said, grinning, the kind of grin a man wears when he's been told exactly how this is going to end and is looking forward to it.

The man behind him moved.

An arm looped round his chest, locking his elbows into his ribs. In the same breath, a second man came at him with the knife raised, three fast strides closing the gap.

Madhava didn't fight the grip. He used it - drove his weight back into the man holding him and kicked both feet up and out. The knife-man folded backward into the mud with a sound like a dropped sack of rice.

The recoil broke the arms round him. That man staggered, lost the road's edge, went down. Madhava went down with him, rolled clear, came up on one knee in the centre of the road. Breathing through his nose. Steady.

Another kick, aimed at his head. He caught the ankle mid-air - watch the hips, not the eyes - and for one long second the man hung there, arms wheeling, a shape against the moon-bright canal with nothing under him at all. Madhava twisted. Pulled. Put him in the canal. The splash was louder than anything that had happened so far, and somewhere off in the dark a dog took it up, barking at nothing it could see.

The rest came together, the way frightened men do, mistaking numbers for a plan.

One from the left. Turned into the blow, sent him into the paddy.
One from behind. Heard the footstep before the breath, pivoted, caught him across the jaw. A crack, wet and short.
One low and close. An elbow. He went down clutching his mouth, a sound coming out of him that wasn't quite a word.

Then a gap. The men drew back a few paces, chests heaving, sizing him up as if he were something other than what they'd been sent to collect a debt from - as if the debt itself had gotten up and started collecting instead.

Blood ran warm down Madhava's eyebrow - the one fist that had gotten through. He wiped it with the back of his wrist and didn't look at it. His hand went inside his coat.

Small pouches. Dark, heavy with liquid. He threw them before the men understood what they were.

They burst across chests, shoulders, one across a startled face. A sharp reek of raw spirit cut through the mud and canal water. The men flinched, wiping at themselves, one of them spitting where a splash had caught his mouth. Not afraid yet.

His hand went in again. Came out with something stranger - short, blunt, built by no factory any of them would know, the metal of it dulled on purpose so it wouldn't catch the moon the way the knives had. He raised it. Pressed the grip.

A string of small fireballs, no bigger than marbles, spat from the barrel.

Where they touched the soaked cloth, the cloth caught.

Screaming. Short. Men tearing at their own shirts, rolling into the wet paddy to smother it, one of them still screaming after the fire itself had gone, as if the sound had outlasted the reason for it. The fire guttered out almost as fast as it had bloomed, pale and clean the way spirit burns rather than oil. Behind it, only raw red patches and the smell of singed hair, sharp and sudden the way it never quite is in the telling of it afterward.

Nobody advanced after that.

Madhava stood where he was, the weapon loose in his hand, and watched them gather their fallen and drag each other back into the fields the way they'd come, the paddy swallowing them the same way it had produced them, rustling long after the last man was out of sight. He didn't follow. He let them go, the way a man lets go of something already broken.

Only when the road was quiet again - truly quiet, the frogs still holding their silence, the dog gone back to whatever had mattered to it before the splash - did his shoulders drop. His ribs ached where a shoulder had driven into him going down. The cut over his eye had found his eye now, stinging, blinking the world into a thin red wash on that side. His shirt was torn at the collar, dark with mud on one side. He put the pouches and the gun away, calm as a man closing a ledger after a bad day's numbers, and started walking again toward the lights of Ryali, listening for the frogs to start again behind him. They didn't, not for a long while.

* * *

At Ajay Hospital, twelve minutes outside the village, the phone at the night desk rang at 10:47. The voice on the other end gave no name.

"Near the ethanol plant, the Ryali road. Men are burned. Send an ambulance."

Then it was gone, the line dead before the attendant could ask anything more - no address beyond the plant itself, no number he could call back, nothing but a voice that had said exactly what it needed to and not one word past it.

Dr. Sukanya was in the residents' room finishing a chart when the attendant found her, still faintly out of breath from having half-run the corridor to reach her. Mid-twenties, sharp-eyed, the kind of doctor the older nurses trusted with things they wouldn't hand a man twice her age, precisely because she asked the right questions in the right order and none that wasted time. She didn't ask who had called, or why an anonymous voice would ring a private hospital instead of the government one closer to the plant. There'd be time for that later, and just now the only clock that mattered was the one counting up from the moment the call had ended.

"Send it now," she said, and reached for her coat, already moving before her arm had found the second sleeve.

The ambulance found them on the field road within the half hour - four men in various states of burn and bruise, sitting or lying where they'd fallen, one of them still trying, weakly, to crawl toward the canal water as if it might do for his skin now what it hadn't managed to do earlier. Past them, walking toward the hospital lights on his own two feet because he'd refused to wait for a second ambulance, a fifth man. Split eyebrow. Mud dried stiff down one side of his shirt. He didn't wave the driver down. He simply kept walking, at the same unhurried pace he might have used on any other night, until the headlights caught him properly and the driver slowed without quite deciding to.

* * *

She had him on a table in the casualty ward twenty minutes later, cleaning the cut above his eye with gauze soaked in saline. He sat still under her hands the way patients rarely did, watching her work rather than the ceiling, his eyes following the small, economical movements of her fingers as if there were something in them worth studying.

"You walked here." Not quite a question.

"It's not far."

"You have four ribs' worth of bruising and you walked here." She taped the last strip of gauze and stepped back to look at him properly - the calm in his face that didn't match the state of his shirt; the way his eyes had followed her hands the whole time instead of drifting to the door, the way frightened men's did, the way even brave men's usually did, if only out of habit. "What happened out there?"

"A disagreement," he said. "It got out of hand."

"Four men came into my casualty ward with burns. That's not a disagreement, Mr-"

"Madhava. Madhava Raju." Plain, no weight on it, though half of Ryali would have known the name - the man who'd put in the biogas plant eight years back, the ethanol plant a year ago, the man half the village's kitchens ran on without knowing it. She'd heard the name before, at some family gathering or another, without ever once putting a face to it - years of hostel and hospital had kept her out of Ryali more than in it. If something clicked now, she didn't let it show. "Suribabu wants to start a liquor business. Needs alcohol for it, cheap, in quantity. Decided I owed it to him. I told him I don't have the capacity - Platanol's output is already committed, most of it to petroleum blending. That was the whole conversation."

"And he sent men to convince you otherwise."

"He doesn't like being told no."

She checked the dressing on his ribs, hands slower than they needed to be, pressing gently along each one and watching his face rather than the bruise for what it might tell her. "The men who came with you. The burns. What happened to them?"

"They came at me with knives. I had a way to stop that, that didn't need a knife of my own." He watched her face for the flinch she'd earned the right to. Didn't find it. "I'm not proud of it. I'd rather it hadn't happened at all."

"How many were there," she asked, not looking up from the bandage, "who came at you?"

"Seven."

She did look up then, just briefly, some calculation running behind her eyes that she didn't share. "And you're the one who walked here."

"I'm the one who walked here."

"You're not what I expected," she said, half to herself, and caught herself saying it, and moved briskly on to the next strip of tape, a faint colour rising at her collar that she didn't acknowledge and he was careful not to notice out loud.

* * *

Down the corridor, in the side room kept for cases the police might eventually ask about, the four burned men lay on adjoining beds while a junior doctor and two nurses worked shifts over what the fire had done to them. They gave their names reluctantly and their reason for being on the Ryali field road at eleven at night not at all, glancing at each other between questions in a way that told the nurses more than the silence itself did. The youngest kept asking, in a thin voice, whether he would scar. Nobody had the patience just then to tell him the truth, and one of the nurses simply patted his good shoulder and told him to rest, which wasn't an answer either but was kinder than one would have been.

* * *

Sukanya came back to Madhava once the ribs were bandaged, ostensibly to check his blood pressure a second time - though it had held steady both times already, a number that told her less about his body than his temperament. She wrapped the cuff round his arm and watched the dial rather than him while she asked the question that had been sitting in her since the side room.

"Why not just give him what he wants? It's alcohol. You produce it. He wants to pay for it - I assume he offered to pay."

"He offered."

"Then why not sell it and be done? You could have saved yourself four ribs and a night in my hospital."

Madhava considered the question longer than she expected, as if it deserved more than the easy answer, his eyes on the far wall rather than her while he worked out how much of it he meant to say out loud. "Because I know what he is," he said finally. "Longer than tonight. Men like Suribabu don't ask for a fair price and stop there. Give him what he wants once, and next season it's more. The season after, it's a share of the plant itself. Say no then, and he doesn't send men to knock you round on a field road - he goes after whoever you'd do anything to protect." Flat, the way he'd talk about tank pressures or delivery schedules. Somehow more convincing than anger would have been. "I'd rather take the beating now, while it's only me standing on that road."

She let the cuff finish its cycle in silence, read the number without really seeing it, unwrapped it slowly, her fingers lingering a beat longer at the velcro than the task required. "You don't trust him."

"I don't trust his mindset. There's a difference. A man can be trusted with money and not trusted with power. Suribabu is the second kind."

"And you decided that eight years ago, or tonight?"

He looked at her properly then, something crossing his face too quickly for her to name it before it was gone again. "Does it matter which."

"No," she said, after a moment. "I suppose it doesn't."

She wrote something on his chart that had nothing to do with his blood pressure, and didn't look up right away when she finished. Outside the curtained bay, the hospital settled into the quiet of past midnight - trolley wheels somewhere down the corridor, a burned man asking again if he would scar, the frogs starting up once more, far off, over the black canal water that had watched the whole thing happen and kept it, for now, to itself.
