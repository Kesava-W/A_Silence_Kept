# Chapter Five: The College Excursion

A week later, a busload of Intermediate students arrived at Platanol's gates for what the notice board back at their college had grandly called an "industrial exposure visit," and what the students themselves were already treating as two days off from regular classes, judging by the volume coming off the bus before it had even fully stopped.

Sukanya had helped arrange it - a favour to the faculty, who knew her from the hospital's blood donation camps, and who'd needed someone locally connected enough to get the plant to say yes on short notice, Madhava being a man who turned down more such requests than he granted, on the grounds that a working ethanol plant was not, in his words, a place for forty teenagers and one clipboard. She came along on the first day more out of habit than necessity, and found herself, by mid-morning, standing at the back of the crowd while Rajesh walked the students through the fermentation tanks with the practised patter of a man who'd given this same talk to visiting officials a dozen times, gesturing at gauges the students mostly weren't looking at and pausing, without fail, at the one point in the tour that reliably got a reaction - the smell.

She drifted, eventually, to where Madhava stood near the control room, arms folded, watching his own plant get lectured about to teenagers with visible pride he wasn't bothering to hide, the way a man watches something he built with his hands still holding up years later.

"You built all this from a dairy," she said.

"From a dairy and a lot of arguing with the bank." He glanced at her, and something in his face shifted - the beginning of a sentence gathering behind it, the kind he didn't often let himself start. "The dairy almost didn't survive its first year. Nobody tells that part to the students."

"Why not?"

"Because Rajesh's version is more inspiring, and he's better at telling it than I am." A pause, longer than the joke needed. "Sukanya, there's something I've been meaning to -"

"Doctor garu!" One of the faculty, waving from across the yard, already mid-crisis over a student who'd wandered too close to the loading bay. "A minute, please!"

Sukanya went, throwing him a look over her shoulder that was half apology and half something she didn't have a name for yet. Madhava watched her go, the sentence he'd been about to say folding itself quietly back down, unfinished the way it had been twice already this month, and told himself, not for the first time that week, that there would be another minute for it later.

* * *

There wasn't, not that day.

On the second morning Rajesh took the students round the plant a second time, deeper into the process now - the distillation columns this time, and the blending yard where the tankers waited to be filled - while Madhava sat closed up in his office with a man from a petroleum blending company who'd come a long way to talk numbers and wasn't in a hurry to leave, the negotiation dragging through two rounds of tea and a lunch neither man finished. Sukanya waited as long as she reasonably could, watching his office door through the visitors' room window between answering a student's questions about whether ethanol production was dangerous, before an emergency call from the hospital pulled her away without so much as a proper goodbye - a note left with the gate guard, hastily written, that Madhava wouldn't find until the office door finally opened an hour later.

She was almost home, the plant's lights well behind her, when her scooty's engine coughed, sputtered, and gave up entirely on the dark stretch of the field road, leaving her stranded with a torch app and a rising sense of how far she still was from anywhere useful, the paddy on either side rustling in a way that felt considerably less charming alone at night than it had from the back of an ambulance weeks earlier.

Madhava's bike came round the bend not ten minutes later - he'd left the plant late himself, the petroleum man finally satisfied and gone - and he didn't need to be asked twice. He had a length of rope out from under his seat before she'd finished explaining what had happened, crouching to check the scooty's engine first out of habit before accepting there was nothing to be done for it out here in the dark, and tied the rope between the two machines with the unhurried competence of a man who fixed things for a living.

"You always carry rope?"

"I always carry several things. You'd be surprised." He said it lightly enough that she couldn't tell, in the dark, whether it was entirely a joke.

He towed her the rest of the way home at a careful, bike-jarring crawl that had them both laughing by the second bend, Sukanya calling out "Nemmadi, nemmadi!" (Slowly, slowly!) and other corrections he didn't need, and he pretending, badly, to take offence at each one.

At her gate, engine off, rope coiled back over his shoulder, he made to leave the way he always did - a nod, a word about the plant, gone before either of them could make more of the moment than the moment already was.

"There's a new film at the theatre in Atreyapuram," Sukanya said, before she could think better of it. "Tomorrow evening. If you're not busy."

He considered it exactly as long as it took to decide he wasn't, in fact, busy. "I'm not busy."

"You didn't even check what film it is."

"Would it change my answer?"

She had no ready answer to that, and didn't try to find one, and he was already turning his bike back toward the road, the rope still over his shoulder, before either of them said anything that might have undone the evening's small, careful progress.

The film, when they went, turned out to be a poor investment on the theatre's part where the two of them were concerned - two hours of a screen neither watched for more than a few minutes at a stretch, each far more interested in the other's face in the flickering light, both pretending, badly, that they weren't, until somewhere past the interval Sukanya gave up the pretence first and simply watched him watching her, and he let her, and neither one looked back at the screen again.
