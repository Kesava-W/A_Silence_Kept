# Chapter Two: Two Homes, Same Night

Prashanthi had the door open before the auto had properly stopped, and she was down the steps before Vijay could tell her to mind her sari on the wet stone. She took her son's arm as if the fight might still be reaching for him, walked him in like a man twice his age, and didn't let go until she'd got him seated in the good chair, the one usually reserved for guests, plumping a cushion behind him that he hadn't asked for and didn't need.

"I'm fine, amma," Madhava said. Not for the first time.

"Ayyo, enti ra idi!" (Oh no, what is this!) "Fine people don't come home with their eyebrow held together by tape." She was already at his shoulder, easing the torn collar back to look at the bandage beneath, tutting at what she found there the way she'd tutted over scraped knees twenty years ago, when the scrapes had come from a mango tree instead of a field road. Her fingers found, out of old habit, the thin red-and-yellow thread still knotted at his wrist - the one she'd tied at Annavaram the year he was born, the year Satyanarayana Swamy had finally answered a vow she'd all but stopped believing would be answered, and had retied there every year since without either of them ever once discussing why. "Seven grown men and my son comes home on his own two feet. What were they thinking, sending seven."

"They weren't thinking much of anything, amma. That was rather the problem."

She didn't laugh at that, though on another night she might have. She only pressed her palm briefly to his cheek, the way she'd done since he was small enough to fit against her hip, and went to find him a change of clothes.

Vijay stood by the door a moment longer than the fuss required, watching, his arms folded in the stillness of a man deciding how much of what he wanted to ask was worth asking tonight.

"Who did this," he said. Quiet. Not really a question either.

"Business trouble." Madhava met his father's eyes and held them, the way he always did when he wanted a subject closed without seeming to close it. "Nothing that won't settle."

"You said that about the loan dispute two years ago, and that one took six months to settle."

"This one will take less."

Vijay looked at him a moment longer - long enough that Madhava understood he wasn't fooled, only choosing, for tonight, not to push - and went to put the kettle on instead, the small domestic sound of it a kind of truce between them, one his father had perfected over thirty years of not asking his son questions the boy plainly didn't want asked.

It was Haritha who said nothing at all. She had come out onto the veranda in her nightdress at the sound of the auto, and had gone very still at the sight of him - the bandage, the stiffness in how he held his ribs, the dried blood still at his hairline that the hospital's gauze hadn't quite hidden. She didn't ask what happened. She sat on the arm of his chair instead, closer than she'd sat since she was small, one hand finding his sleeve and staying there, and stayed through her mother's fussing and her father's tea and the whole slow hour of the house settling back to quiet, saying almost nothing, watching him as if watching would keep something from happening twice. Once, when Prashanthi left the room for the ointment, she leaned her head briefly against his good shoulder, and he let her, and neither of them said what either of them was thinking about a night eight years gone that this one didn't resemble nearly as much as it felt like it should.

"Go to sleep, Haritha," he told her finally, gently, when even Prashanthi had run out of things to check on him. "I'm not going anywhere."

"You'd better not be." It came out lighter than she meant it, and she seemed to hear that herself, and softened it further with a small, tired smile before she went, reluctantly, glancing back once from the doorway of her room before she shut it.

Madhava sat alone after that, in the good chair, in the quiet the house made after midnight. His ribs ached in time with his pulse. He should have gone to bed. Instead he sat a while longer, his eyes resting on his sister's closed door - a moment longer than the hour or his injuries called for - before he finally got up, put out the light, and went to lie awake in a dark that had never once, in his whole life, frightened him.

* * *

Across the village, in a smaller house with a tulasi plant by the step and a scooter parked crooked against the wall, Sirisha was still awake when Sukanya came in, changed out of her hospital clothes, and sat down to the plate her mother had kept covered, the rice gone a little cool but the sambar reheated twice already, judging by the smell of it.

"Enti idi, inta ratri?" (What's this, so late at night?) "Who was it, this ambulance you sent off at eleven at night?" Sirisha asked, more curious than accusing, already ladling a second spoon of sambar onto her daughter's plate before Sukanya had asked for it. News in Ryali travelled faster than ambulances did - she'd have heard some version of it from the neighbour two houses down before Sukanya's own auto had turned onto their lane.

"Madhava. From Platanol." Sukanya kept her eyes on her plate. "Some men attacked him near the plant. He's alright - a few stitches, some bruising."

"Attacked him how? For what reason?"

"Business trouble. Something about alcohol supply." Sukanya reached for the water, keeping her voice level the way she kept it for family members asking after patients she wasn't at liberty to discuss further, though there was no rule of confidentiality standing between her and her own mother, only the sense that she hadn't yet decided how much of tonight she wanted to hand over for discussion at this table.

From the next room, where he was meant to be asleep and clearly was not, Krishna's voice floated out, delighted to have something to hold over his sister before she'd even finished her rice. "Ayyo! Akka ki prema vachesindi anukunta!" (Oh! I think our sister's fallen in love!) "Since when does our doctor akka lose sleep over a stranger's stitches?"

"He's not a stranger, Krishna. Everyone in Ryali knows his plant."

"That's not what I asked." A pause, theatrical, the kind he'd perfected over years of needling her across exactly this table. "You went yourself. You didn't send Ravindra or one of the juniors. You went."

"It was my shift."

"It's always somebody's shift. You don't always go."

"Go to sleep." She said it quickly - too quickly, a half-beat faster than the comment deserved - and reached for the water instead of answering further, and from the next room came a low, satisfied laugh that told her exactly how well her brother had understood what her hurry meant.

Manikanta, at the head of the table, said nothing at all. He had noticed, the way he noticed most things without remarking on them, how carefully his daughter had chosen those two sentences, how little of Madhava's plant and how much of Madhava himself she'd managed not to say, and how her hand had gone still over her plate for the length of Krishna's question before it moved again. He watched her finish her plate in the same careful, unhurried silence, and let the matter go for tonight - the way a man lets a fishing line run a little longer before he decides whether there's anything on the other end worth pulling in.
