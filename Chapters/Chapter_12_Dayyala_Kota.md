# Chapter Twelve: Dayyala Kota

The bungalow at Dayyala Kota had stood empty for longer than anyone in Ryali could say for certain - long enough that children dared each other to touch its gate after dark, long enough that the stories about it had outlived whoever first told them. Its windows were black sockets. Its walls had given up most of their plaster to the monsoons, and the generator someone had dragged in for tonight sat incongruous and modern against all that rot, coughing black smoke into a room that hadn't held a working light in a decade.

They brought Sukanya in under a single flickering bulb strung from that generator, Suribabu's remaining men ringed around her with whatever weapons they still owned, and Suribabu himself standing close enough that she could smell the arrack on him, stronger tonight than she'd have expected from a man who claimed to have planned this carefully.

"You should know," he said, almost gently, crouching slightly to bring his face level with hers where she sat, "what your husband married into. Eight years ago, fifteen men died in this house in one night. Because of me." He let that sit, watching her face for the fear he expected. "I made sure this village believed that, Doctor garu, and every man who's crossed me since has done it knowing what I was capable of. So you understand what happens here, if he doesn't come for you the way I know he will."

Sukanya had been silent since the car. She was not silent now. Months of silence had bought her exactly this - one room full of frightened men, and a truth she could finally afford to spend.

"Those fifteen men," she said, and something in her voice made even the nearest of Suribabu's men shift his weight, "were killed by Madhava. For what they did to his sister." Her voice didn't shake. "You've spent months telling this village a story that was never yours. You didn't kill those men, Suribabu. You couldn't. And the four who went missing after that - the ones your own men have spent weeks searching the canals for - he killed those too. Buried them somewhere you'll never find, the same night he nearly died doing it."

"You're lying. A frightened woman will say anything."

"Am I frightened?" She held his eyes, and whatever he saw there wasn't fear, and it cost him something to keep looking. "Ask yourself why four of your men have gone into fields around this village and not come out. Ask yourself why the one man you couldn't buy or break was never once afraid of your fifteen dead men, not for a single day. You built your whole reputation on a story that belonged to someone else, and you're about to find out exactly how badly that borrowed reputation is going to cost you."

The generator's bulb flickered once, twice.

Around her, Suribabu's men went very still - the particular stillness of men recalculating, too late, exactly what kind of night they'd walked into. One of them took an unconscious half-step back toward the doorway, toward whatever light still existed outside it.

"She's lying," Suribabu said again, to his men now rather than to her, and heard, even as he said it, how little conviction was left in his own voice.

The bulb went out.

* * *

Madhava came through the gate already inside the dark, gloves on, a blade in one hand, and to him, none of it was dark at all.

A shape moved to his left. He was already there.

Steel found flesh. Short. Final.

A man behind him swung wide at nothing, at a target that had already moved. Madhava turned inside the swing and ended it.

Someone screamed for light. There was no light coming.

"Where is he - I can't see him, I can't see anything -"

The voice cut off mid-sentence.

He didn't hurry. He didn't need to. Every man in that room was fighting a night he couldn't see through, against one who saw all of it, and the space between them closed and closed again, blow after blow, each one carrying the same single thought behind it: that it wasn't only her life on the ground here tonight, but the child she carried too, and there was no version of tonight where he stopped before every threat to either of them had stopped moving.

Suribabu tried to run. Didn't get far. The last thing he understood, in the half-second there was time to understand anything, was that the boast he'd never actually earned had finally met the man it actually belonged to.

Then, at last, stillness. Real stillness, the kind that follows rather than precedes.

A hand found hers in the dark - the second time it had happened to her in a clearing like this one, and she knew it before it had even closed around her fingers.

"Sukanya."

"I'm here." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I'm here. Both of us are."

His hand moved, careful in the dark, to rest briefly against the curve of her stomach, as if he needed to confirm with his own palm what his ears had already told him - that nothing tonight had reached that far. He said nothing. He didn't need to.

He led her out of the bungalow the way he'd led her out of the dark once before, her hand in his, stepping over what the darkness had hidden from her and hadn't hidden from him at all, and neither of them looked back at Dayyala Kota again.
