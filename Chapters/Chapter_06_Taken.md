# Chapter Six: Taken

Two weeks later, Suribabu heard from one of his men - the kind of man whose job was mostly to watch and report, and who'd been watching Platanol's gate on and off for exactly this sort of news - that the doctor from Ajay Hospital and the Platanol owner had been seen together at the Atreyapuram theatre, and more than once since.

Suribabu smiled. It was not a pleasant smile. A man who wouldn't bend for money, he'd found before, would very often bend for someone he couldn't afford to lose.

"Find out where she works. Her shifts. When she's alone." He said it without heat, the way he gave most of his instructions, a man ordering supplies rather than planning a night. "And find men who won't ask questions about who she is before they take her."

* * *

Madhava was on the night shift at the plant, going over the log with Rajesh, when his phone rang with Ajay Hospital's number on the screen. Not Sukanya's number. The hospital's front desk, breathless, telling him Dr. Sukanya had been taken - forced into a car outside the staff quarters by men nobody recognised, no plate number anyone had thought to catch in the confusion, gone before the security guard had even finished shouting - and that they hadn't known who else to call.

His heart didn't race so much as go very cold and very fast at once. He was already moving toward the door - Rajesh half-rising from his stool, asking what had happened, getting no answer - when the second call came through, this one from a number he didn't recognise, and a voice he did.

"One hour," Suribabu said, without preamble. "Decide whether you'll give me the alcohol, or decide what happens to her instead."

"If you touch her -"

"I haven't decided yet what I'll do. That's rather the point, Raju garu. One hour." The line went dead before Madhava could say anything more, and there was, in the end, nothing more he'd have said to it that would have changed a single minute of the hour now running.

He didn't need to answer him. He was already opening Find My on his phone, watching a small dot resolve itself somewhere off the main road, out past the coconut groves, in the direction of ground that had no business having anyone on it after dark.

He was on his bike before the call had finished ringing out, Rajesh's voice calling after him lost somewhere in the noise of the engine.

* * *

The place Suribabu's men had taken her to was thick with trees, the kind of ground the moonlight never fully reached even on a clear night, and tonight the clouds had swallowed what moon there was besides.

Sukanya sat where they'd left her, wrists bound to a low branch, listening to the men who'd brought her talking low a few feet away - four of them, by the voices, one of them laughing at something the others didn't find as funny - unable to see more than the vague shift of their shapes against slightly less-dark shapes. She kept her breathing even, the way she'd trained herself to for patients in worse states than her own, and told herself, twice, three times, that panicking would spend something she might need later and get her nothing now.

Then, without warning, the dark changed.

Footsteps, first - circling, not hers. Then a sound she couldn't place at all: cloth, breath, something heavy hitting the ground. A blade caught the last thread of light there was, and was gone again. A cry, cut off before it finished being a cry. More weight hitting the ground. One man. Then another. Then nothing.

Silence.

She didn't move. Didn't dare to. And then a hand found hers in the dark - warm, sure, unmistakably a hand she'd have known blind, which, just then, she was.

She understood, in that pitch-black clearing, more than she would ever say aloud to anyone - not the how of it, not yet, but the shape of what he must be, what he must have just done, and done, she was certain even then, for no reason but her.

"Madhava."

No answer. Just his hand working, quick and sure even in the dark, at the rope round her wrists.

"Are you -"

Before she could find a single word more for any of it, his grip went slack, and he went down beside her without a sound.

* * *

She got him into the car she flagged down on the main road herself, half-carrying him the last stretch, refusing every question the frightened driver tried to ask beyond the name of the hospital, and she told no one, that night or after, what she had pieced together in the dark at Suribabu's clearing.

Whatever he was. Whatever he had done, tonight and perhaps before tonight. It mattered less to her, driving through the dark toward Ajay Hospital with his head in her lap and his blood on her hands, than whether he would still be breathing when they arrived.

The why of it could wait. It would have to.
