# Chapter Four: What the Rumours Carry

A week later, in the lull between the morning's patients and the afternoon's, Chitra caught Sukanya in the nurses' station with two cups of tea and a look that meant she wasn't leaving without an answer.

"That plant owner," she said, setting one cup down in front of Sukanya without being asked, and settling herself onto the stool opposite with the air of someone who had cleared her schedule for this. "The one from the burns case. Who is he, exactly? Ryali's full of business owners and I've never once seen you write extra notes on any of their charts."

"I don't write extra notes."

"You wrote a full paragraph about his blood pressure trend on a man who came in for a bandage. Nobody does that for a bandage, Sukanya."

Sukanya took the tea if only to have something to do with her hands. "Madhava Raju. He started a dairy business and a biogas plant, GasBio, must be eight years back now. Then the ethanol plant - Platanol - just over a year ago."

"And the burns?"

"That's the ethanol plant's doing, in a manner of speaking." Sukanya turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "A man named Suribabu wants to start a liquor business. He wanted Madhava's alcohol supply cheap, and Madhava said no, and Suribabu doesn't take no well."

"Abbabba!" (Good heavens!) "So he sent seven men to change his mind, and one man walked away from all seven of them on his own feet." Chitra let that sit a moment, watching her friend over the rim of her own cup. "That's not the kind of man you write extra paragraphs about because you're worried for him, is it. That's the kind of man you write extra paragraphs about because you can't stop thinking about him."

"It's not like that."

"Sukanya. Your eyes are doing something right now that they don't do for the diabetic foot cases."

"They're not doing anything."

"They're wet, is what they're doing." Chitra's voice dropped, gentler now, the teasing gone out of it, and she reached across to still the cup Sukanya kept turning. "What does he mean to you?"

Sukanya opened her mouth, and nothing came, and she closed it again, and looked very hard at her tea until the moment passed and a nurse called Chitra away to a patient who actually needed her, leaving the second question hanging in the small station along with the steam off two cooling cups.

* * *

Suribabu, for his part, had decided that if Madhava would not be bought, he would be frightened instead - and frightening a man in Ryali was best done through the mouths of other men, because a rumour, unlike a threat, could never be traced back far enough to answer for itself.

It started as most rumours did - a word dropped at the toddy shop, another at the bus stand, always by one of Suribabu's own, never traced back to him directly. At the toddy shop it went round in the low, satisfied voice men use for gossip they've been paid to spread and are enjoying regardless. "You know Dayyala Kota, that bungalow, the haunted one," one of Suribabu's men said, leaning in over a half-finished glass to a table of men who hadn't asked. "Eight years back. Fifteen bodies in one night. Throats opened, every one." He let the silence do its work before he finished it. "That was Suribabu garu. He doesn't talk about it. But I've heard it from someone who was there." Nobody at the table had heard it from someone who was there either, not really, but by morning three more tellings had passed it further down the lane, each one a little more certain of itself than the last, the way rumours in Ryali always grew more confident the further they travelled from anyone who actually knew.

It reached Madhava through Rajesh, who brought it to him at the plant with the careful, apologetic air of a man passing along gossip he didn't fully believe himself, standing in the office doorway turning his cap in his hands rather than sitting down.

"There's talk going round, sir. About Suribabu." Rajesh hesitated. "About Dayyala Kota. The old case, the fifteen men. People are saying he was the one who did it."

Madhava listened to the whole of it without his face changing, and when Rajesh finished, he almost laughed - a short, dry sound, nothing like amusement.

"Suribabu," he said, "doesn't have the stomach to kill a chicken himself, let alone fifteen men." He went back to the ledger in front of him, running a finger down a column of figures that hadn't changed since Rajesh walked in. "Let him talk. Talk doesn't cost me anything."

"Should I tell the men not to repeat it, sir? Some of them are getting nervous. Suribabu's crowd, they're saying he's not a man to cross now."

"Let them say what they like. It changes nothing about tomorrow's shift." He looked up, briefly, something unreadable passing behind his eyes before it was gone again. "A rumour is only dangerous to the man who's afraid of it, Rajesh. I'm not."

Rajesh nodded and left it there, and never once thought to ask - not that day, and not for a long while after - how Madhava could sound so entirely certain of what fifteen dead men in Dayyala Kota had or hadn't required, when the rest of Ryali had spent eight years certain of nothing at all.
