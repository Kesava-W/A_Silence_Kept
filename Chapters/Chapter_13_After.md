# Chapter Thirteen: After

Devendra's case closed quietly, the way cold cases sometimes did when there was simply nothing left to chase.

The bodies of the first four missing men were never found, buried wherever Madhava had put them months before, and the scene at Dayyala Kota, when the police finally reached it two days after the fact on an anonymous tip, read exactly like what it had been made to look like - a falling-out among criminals, goons and the man who'd led them killing each other in the dark over some dispute nobody left alive could explain. No prints that mattered. No witness willing, or able, to say otherwise.

Devendra walked the bungalow himself before the scene was cleared, the way he always did, looking for the one detail that refused to fit the story everyone else was already prepared to write down. He found nothing that argued with it. Only the same old rot in the walls, and a generator that had run out of fuel sometime before dawn, and, somewhere underneath all of it, the faint, familiar sense that he was standing in a room that had kept exactly this kind of secret before.

He took the old folder out of his drawer one last time, read through it the way he had a hundred times before, and put it back for good. He never did find the man who could see in the dark. Some nights, driving home past the turning for Dayyala Kota, he wondered if he'd already met him - shaken his hand, thanked his wife for her patience answering questions at a hospital bedside months before - and decided, each time, that it was a thought better left where the case now lived: closed, and unproven, and quiet. He told the young constable, when the boy asked one last time whether the file should stay open, that some cases didn't close so much as they simply stopped needing an answer, and that a man learned, eventually, which kind was which.

* * *

Years before any of it - before Platanol, before Sukanya, before a field road that would one day become the story people told about him - Madhava Raju was eleven years old, and Dayyala Kota was still just an old empty house, not yet a story anyone told.

Every child in Ryali knew the dare even if most of them never tried it: touch the gate after dark and run. Madhava went on a night the whole house had gone to sleep around him, out through the back door on feet that knew which floorboards to avoid, because he had decided, in the stubborn way of eleven-year-olds, that he was done being the one who ran before he'd even touched it.

The gate gave easier than the stories made it sound. He touched it, and didn't run, and found that not running had left him standing at the open mouth of the house itself, with nothing behind him now but the decision already made.

Inside, it should have been nothing - four walls he couldn't see, air thick with old rain and older rot. It wasn't nothing. He could see the collapsed roof beam, the rat holes at the skirting, the whole ruined shape of the room, as plainly as his own courtyard at noon. He didn't understand it. At eleven, there was still room in him for wonder that a grown man usually has to make room for something else.

He was still deciding whether that frightened him more than the house did when he understood he was no longer alone in the room.

There was no sound to it. No shift in the dark a grown man's instincts would have caught first. Only, where a moment before there had been nothing but a collapsed beam, a shape - and a light with it, though light was the wrong word for something that reached no wall and threw no shadow and seemed to come from nowhere outside itself. Madhava would spend the rest of his life declining to describe it, even to himself, past the plainest facts of it. That it looked at him. That it smiled - not kindly, not unkindly, the way the sun doesn't mean anything by rising - and asked nothing of him, and gave him nothing he could have named, and was gone before he'd finished being afraid.

He walked home the way he'd come. The dark was no different behind him than it had been in front. He let himself in through the same back door, lay awake until the window went grey, and never once, from that night on, doubted what he'd have to carry alone.

He told no one. Not his mother, tying his thread fresh that same week for reasons that had nothing to do with what he'd seen. Not, decades later, a wife who would come to know nearly everything else about him. What grew up around Dayyala Kota in the years after - lights no one could explain, a boy from the Raju house who came back from a dare gone quiet for a week, and then, much later, fifteen dead men in one terrible night - none of it, to Madhava, was ever the beginning of the story. He already knew where that began. He simply never said.

Years later, Devendra would stand in that same room and feel it too, and never know why he was right.

* * *

Six months later, in a bright ward at Ajay Hospital that Sukanya knew better than any room in her own house, she held their daughter for the first time, still worn through with the work of the last several hours, and looked up to find Madhava standing over the two of them with an expression she had genuinely never seen on his face before - not the composure he carried into everything else, not even the careful joy of the pregnancy test six months back, but something unguarded, entirely undone.

The hospital's ethanol had been coming off Platanol's own floor for months now, ever since she'd mentioned once, in passing, how much cheaper and closer it would be than their usual distributor. She'd half forgotten saying it until the MD thanked her for the introduction, one ordinary morning, as though it had been her doing all along.

"She has your hands," Sukanya said, exhausted, smiling.

"Amma bangaram vachesindi." (My darling girl has arrived.) "She has your stubbornness already. Wouldn't stop crying until she got exactly where she wanted to be." He sat carefully on the edge of the bed, one finger against his daughter's impossibly small palm, and she gripped it before either of them expected her to. Madhava laughed - a real one, low and rough at the edges - and didn't try, this time, to hold anything back at all.

It was the vow-thread at his own wrist that caught Sukanya's eye, not for the first time that week - the one Prashanthi had shown her once, laughing, the story of a temple and a young mother's desperate vow. Madhava had never once brought it up himself. Something rare crossed his face just now, something she couldn't remember seeing there before, and told her what she'd never quite let herself think in words: that whatever he believed he was, he'd believed it long before he'd had any reason to trust her with it, and had carried it alone, in silence, his entire life. He didn't say it, even now. She'd long since stopped needing him to.

In the corridor outside, Prashanthi was already fussing at a nurse - "Jagrattaga chudandi, pasipilla kada!" (Look after her carefully, she's a newborn!) - about whether the ward was warm enough for a newborn. She was already planning, though she'd said nothing of it yet, the trip to Annavaram before the year was out - the same journey she'd made the year Madhava was born, when she'd stood before Satyanarayana Swamy and asked, in the plain desperate wording of a new mother, for a son who would always be safe, whatever shape that safety needed to take. She'd spent years telling herself it was only a mother's superstition, the story she told herself about a boy who never once cried at the dark, never once asked for a lamp by his bed, and grew into a man who somehow always saw trouble coming before anyone else in the room did. Tonight, seeing her son with his daughter, she believed every word of it again.

Vijay stood a little apart from her with his hands clasped behind his back and an expression suspiciously close to tears he'd deny later if anyone asked.

Haritha, pressed to the window glass before she'd even been told she could go in, watched her brother with his daughter and thought, not for the first time that year, that whatever he had done and would never speak of - and she had her own guesses, closer to the truth than she'd ever tell him, about a night when she was seventeen and frightened and he had simply made sure she never had to be that frightened again - this, this quiet, undone man, was the truest thing she'd ever seen him be.

Outside the window, Ryali went on the way it always had - canals catching the evening light, the coconut groves stirring in a wind coming off the Godavari, a village that would never know the whole of what its own fields had carried, some nights, in the dark. Not even Rajesh, who ran the night shift most weeks now and had long since stopped wondering why his employer never seemed to need a torch.
