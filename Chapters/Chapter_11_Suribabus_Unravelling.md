# Chapter Eleven: Suribabu's Unravelling

Suribabu had built what little he had on other men's fear long before Madhava ever refused him anything.

It was an old pattern with him - a partner brought in on generous terms that turned less generous once the business was running, a share renegotiated at the point of a threat rather than a handshake, and, more than once over the years, a wife or a brother or a son gone missing for a day or two until the partner in question remembered how reasonable Suribabu's terms had sounded after all. It had worked for him for the better part of two decades. It was how he'd built enough, eventually, to look at Madhava's growing ethanol plant and decide he wanted a piece of it too - cheap, and on his terms, the way he wanted everything.

Madhava, who'd heard the stories the same as everyone else in Ryali, had simply said no, and meant it, and that no was the first thing in a long while that Suribabu hadn't been able to bend by asking a second time, louder.

His missing men didn't help him. Four gone after the failed kidnapping, all at once, in a night he still didn't fully understand, and a village that noticed things it didn't say aloud began, slowly, to notice that Suribabu's men went out and didn't always come back.

* * *

Narayana Rao had been one of Suribabu's oldest arrangements - a rice mill on the Atreyapuram road, a fifty-fifty partnership on paper that had quietly become sixty-forty, then seventy-thirty, over eleven years of Suribabu finding new reasons why his share needed revisiting. He came to the house himself this time, rather than sending word, and Suribabu, seeing who it was from the veranda, felt the first real unease he'd allowed himself in weeks.

"Ika chaalu, Suribabu garu." (Enough now, Suribabu garu.) "I've come to tell you myself," Narayana Rao said, not sitting when he was offered a chair, "rather than have you hear it from someone else first. The mill's accounts are being redrawn. Fifty-fifty, from the first of next month. As they should have stayed from the start."

"We had an arrangement."

"We had an arrangement you renegotiated at the point of a threat, three separate times, the last one involving my own nephew going missing for two days until I saw reason." Narayana Rao's voice didn't rise, which somehow made it worse than shouting would have. "I saw reason because I was afraid of you, Suribabu garu. I'm telling you plainly, because you'd find out anyway - I'm no longer afraid of you."

"You should be careful what you're no longer afraid of."

"Should I?" The older man looked at him a long moment, something almost like pity in it. "Four men of yours have gone into the fields around this village in the last two months and not come back out. Nobody knows where. Nobody, including you, from what I hear. A man who can't keep his own people safe isn't a man whose threats I need to lie awake over any longer." He turned to go, then paused at the door. "Fifty-fifty, from next month. If you'd like to argue the figure with a lawyer instead of a nephew, my son knows a good one."

He left Suribabu standing in his own front room with nothing to say to an empty doorway, and by evening the story of it had made its way, in the loose, gossip-carried way such stories moved through Ryali, to three other men who'd been waiting, quietly, for exactly this kind of weakness in exactly this kind of man.

One by one, over the following weeks, agreements Suribabu had once enforced with a phone call and a threat simply stopped being honoured. Payments arrived late, then not at all. A man who had spent twenty years being feared discovered, in the space of a season, what it felt like to be merely disliked - and disliked men in Ryali did not stay in business long.

* * *

By the time he called what remained of his men together in the back room above the toddy shop he half-owned, there were four of them left willing to sit in a room with him at all, and even they sat further from him than they once had.

"Four of ours. Gone." Suribabu said it flatly, laying his palms on the table as if the wood itself owed him an answer. "Not run off. Not arrested - I'd have heard. Gone, the way a man goes when somebody decides he shouldn't come back."

"We've searched the canals twice over," one of the men said. "The old wells past Dayyala Kota. Nothing. Whoever's doing this isn't leaving anything to find."

"Then find who is doing it."

"With respect, Suribabu garu -" the youngest of the four, the one who still had the most to lose by staying, chose his words with visible care - "half the village thinks it's you doing it. The rumour you started yourself, about the fifteen men, years back - it's come back round on you. People are saying maybe you're the one who can't be trusted with his own business partners, not the other way round."

"That rumour was meant to frighten Raju into selling me his alcohol. It was never meant to -" Suribabu stopped himself, some private calculation crossing his face. "It doesn't matter what it was meant to do. What matters is what's true now. My partners are walking away. My men are disappearing. And Raju still hasn't given me one drop of what I asked for eight months ago."

"So what do we do?"

Suribabu was quiet a long moment, turning something over that none of the remaining four much liked the look of once it settled on his face. "We tried frightening him through the alcohol. We tried frightening him through his men. Neither worked, because he doesn't frighten for himself." He looked up. "But he married the doctor. And she's carrying now - the whole village knows it. A man like that, who won't bend for his own skin, might bend for hers. Especially with a child coming."

"Suribabu garu, taking her again, this soon, this public - if it goes wrong -"

"It went wrong the first time because we were careless and gave him an hour to think." Suribabu's voice had gone quiet in a way that made even his own men uneasy. "This time we don't ask. We don't wait an hour. We take her to Dayyala Kota, where there's nowhere for anyone to come at us from more than one direction, and we make sure that when he comes - and he will come - he understands exactly what he's walking into before he walks into it."

Desperate now, and running low on the only currency he'd ever really traded in, Suribabu did the only thing left that had ever worked for him before. He took Sukanya a second time - pregnant now, and every kitchen in Ryali already talking about it, taken from outside her own gate in broad daylight by whatever men he had left willing to follow him still - and had her driven out to the old bungalow at Dayyala Kota.

Not to bargain with this time. Only to frighten her, and through her, to frighten the one man in Ryali who had never once been afraid of him.
