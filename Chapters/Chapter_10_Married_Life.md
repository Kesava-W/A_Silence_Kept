# Chapter Ten: Married Life

Two weeks into their marriage, the lamp turned low but not yet out, Sukanya lay with her hand resting flat on his bare chest, studying him in the dim light with an ease that hadn't been there on the wedding night.

"You were staring at me this morning," she said. "By the kitchen window, while I was drying my hair. Rajesh had to say your name twice before you heard him."

"No, I wasn't." He caught her hand before it could pinch him for the memory.

"You were. You nearly walked into the doorframe on your way out."

"Fine." He gave it up plainly, no fight left in it. "Seeing you after you've washed your hair, drying it out like that in the mornings - it does something to me I have to actively fight off most days now, because I have a plant to run and no business being late to it."

She went faintly pink and pressed her palm harder against his chest, half a shove. "You've never once said that to me before tonight."

"I've thought it since about the second week I knew you. I don't say most of what I think, in case you hadn't noticed."

"I had noticed." She traced a slow line along his collarbone, thoughtful now rather than teasing. "I used to think it was because you didn't have much to say. Now I think it's because you say almost everything with your eyes instead, and expect the rest of us to keep up."

"Have you been keeping up?"

"Mostly." A small, private smile. "It took me a while."

"And if I decided, one day, to make sure you were late?" she said, returning to the earlier thread with a glint that hadn't been there a moment before.

"Then," he said, perfectly serious, "the neighbours are going to have questions about the noise."

"Ayyayyo!" (Oh my!) She clapped a hand over her own mouth, scandalised and delighted in equal measure, then swatted him properly on the arm for it, and he caught her wrist and kissed her forehead instead of apologising, and she was still laughing, quietly, into his shoulder, long after the lamp had been put out.

* * *

Some weeks into their marriage, putting away a stack of ironed shirts, Sukanya found what she hadn't been looking for - a false-bottomed shelf at the back of Madhava's cupboard, and in it, wrapped in cloth, small dark pouches she recognised for what they must have done to four burned men months ago, and beside them, a strange short weapon she'd never laid eyes on before and, looking at it now, wasn't surprised by at all - the same quiet certainty that had first taken root the night of a hospital bandage and a question about trust she'd never gotten the whole answer to.

She didn't gasp. Didn't drop them. She simply stood there with the shirts still over one arm and waited for him to come find her standing over the open cupboard.

He didn't lie, and didn't look surprised that she'd found them, either - only came and sat on the edge of the bed and told her, plainly, that he'd built them himself, that he'd kept a set ready long before that night on the field road, ever since he'd first sensed Suribabu's men taking too long an interest in the plant's fence line.

"You built these. Yourself."

"I've always been better with my hands than most people expect from a man who runs a business now." He said it without pride, a simple fact laid down between them. "The pouches are ethanol, mostly - straight off Platanol's own floor, before it ever reaches a tanker. I thickened it myself, so it clings instead of running off the second it lands. Catches quick. Doesn't wash off in a hurry once it's on you. The gun took longer to get right."

"You said you'd kept a set ready. Since before the field road." She sat down slowly on the edge of the bed, a careful distance from him, watching his face rather than the shelf now. "You knew they'd come for you. Before they did."

"I suspected." He said it the way he said most things, without embellishment. "I've always preferred to be ready rather than sorry."

"How long have you been ready, Madhava? Since Suribabu, or since before him?"

He didn't answer that one straightaway, and she watched something move behind his eyes that she'd seen only once before, in a dark clearing with her wrists still raw from rope. "Since before him," he said finally. "A long time before him."

Something in it caught at her, and it took her a moment to place why. A long time. Eight years, near enough, was the figure that came to mind unbidden - the same eight years she'd heard attached to him at every family gathering since she was old enough to be told who owned which plant in Ryali, a boy barely into his twenties who'd put his own money into a biogas business instead of a house or a wedding, with a younger sister then still finishing her last year of school, for reasons nobody in her hearing had ever quite explained.

She let the silence sit between them a moment, weighing how far to walk down the road both answers had opened, and decided, watching his face, not tonight. "Is there anything in that cupboard I should be frightened of?"

"No." He met her eyes fully for the first time since she'd asked. "Never of you. Never because of you either, if I can help it."

She looked at the pouches a moment longer, and then closed the shelf, gently, the way you close a door on a room you've decided not to need to enter again just now. She asked him nothing further that night - not what else he'd used them for, not what else he was ready for, not the shape of the question that had been sitting, half-formed, since a hand had found hers once in the pitch dark - and he didn't offer, and something between them settled into a shape it would keep from then on: not the whole truth spoken, but enough of it trusted.

* * *

A few months after that, in the same bedroom, Sukanya woke him before dawn with a pregnancy test in one hand and an expression he'd never seen on her before - somewhere between terror and joy, unable yet to settle on either.

"Madhava, le le!" (Madhava, get up, get up!) "Madhava. Madhava, wake up." She was sitting up already, the test held out in front of her like something that might change its answer if she looked away. "Look at this. Look."

He surfaced slowly, then not slowly at all, sitting up and taking the test from her hand, turning it toward the grey light coming in past the curtain. He looked at the two lines a long moment, and then at her, and the sound he made was not a word at all, just something caught halfway between a laugh and his own breath going out of him.

"Say something," she said, half-laughing herself now, half still bracing for whatever his face was about to decide.

"I don't - " He stopped, tried again. "I've never once in my life not known what to say next. Give me a minute."

"You have exactly one minute, and then I'm going to assume the worst."

He didn't need the minute. He had her up off the bed and off her feet before she could say anything further, spinning her once in the small room until she begged him, laughing, to put her down before he did the baby some injury nine months early.

He put her down, carefully, both hands still at her waist as if he didn't quite trust the floor to hold her weight on its own just yet. He kept his hand flat against her stomach for a long time after, as if he could already feel something there, and said nothing at all - for once, not because he was holding something back, but because there was nothing he could have said that would have carried even half of it.

"You're crying," she said softly, reaching up to touch his face.

"I'm aware."

"I've never seen you cry. Not even at the wedding."

"I was saving it," he said, his voice not entirely steady, "for something worth it."
