# Chapter Seven: The Cold Case

Two days later, in the long grey hour after visiting hours when the ward finally quietened, Madhava lay still unconscious, the machines beside him marking out a steady, patient rhythm that Sukanya had stopped needing to check every five minutes only sometime around dawn, though she checked it anyway, out of a habit that had stopped being purely professional some days back and hadn't quite told her when.

His parents had gone home for the night at her insistence, Prashanthi close to swaying on her feet with exhaustion, Vijay not much better though he'd never have admitted it, both of them made to promise they'd eat something before they slept. Sukanya stayed, in the chair pulled close to the bed, watching his face for any change and finding none, his hand loosely under hers on the blanket the way it had been for the better part of two days now.

That was where Devendra found her.

He came in plain clothes, unhurried, a constable a step behind him with a notebook already out, and introduced himself with the particular courtesy policemen used on people they weren't yet sure were witnesses or something else. "Dr. Sukanya? Sub-Inspector Devendra. A few questions, if you don't mind. About the night Mr. Raju was brought in."

"Of course. Though I should tell you, I've already given a statement to the constable who came the first night."

"I've read it." He drew up the second chair without being asked, the constable remaining standing. "I'd like to hear it again, if that's alright. Sometimes a second telling remembers something the first one didn't have room for."

She told him what she had already decided, days ago, to tell anyone who asked. That Madhava had been struck from behind by one of the men who'd taken her, that he'd gone down and stayed down, that his attackers must have believed him dead and left him where he'd fallen. She said it steadily, without hesitation, the practised calm of a doctor giving a case history - and none of it, strictly, a lie, only carefully short of the whole of it.

"You didn't see who struck him."

"It was dark. I couldn't see much of anything."

"But you heard something. A struggle, you said, in your first statement."

"Sounds. Movement. I couldn't tell you how many men, or what happened to each of them." That much, at least, was true down to the letter, if not the spirit of it.

Devendra wrote nothing down. He simply watched her, the way policemen learn to watch, his pen resting capped against the notebook rather than moving across it, and said, almost as an aside, "Four of Suribabu's men have been missing for two days now. Since that same night." He let that sit a moment, his eyes not leaving her face. "You didn't mention that in your first statement either. That there might have been more than the two men who took you and the one who came for you."

"I told you what I saw. I didn't see how many there were."

"No." He said it mildly, the way a man agrees with something he doesn't quite believe, filing the disagreement away for later rather than pressing it now. "If you remember anything else, Doctor. Anything at all. My number's on the card." He set it on the bedside table, beside the water jug, and rose to go.

She nodded. He left the way he'd come, in no hurry, and didn't look convinced by anything she'd said, though he hadn't looked unconvinced either - a policeman's skill, she thought, watching the door close behind him, of leaving a room having given away exactly nothing about what he believed.

* * *

Back at the station, the young constable who'd stood silent through the whole interview waited until they were back in the jeep before he finally asked the question he'd clearly been sitting on the entire drive.

"Sir, can I ask - why are we pushing so hard on this one? On paper it's four missing men, and men like that go missing all the time. Nobody's filed a complaint. Nobody's family has even come asking."

Devendra didn't answer right away. He drove another minute in silence, past the turning that led, if you followed it far enough and knew which fork to take, to a bungalow the constable had grown up being warned away from.

"Eight years ago," he said finally, "in that bungalow out at Dayyala Kota - the one they say is haunted - we found fifteen men dead in one night. Throats opened. No one ever charged."

"I've heard the stories. Half the station has. Nobody talks about it much."

"No, they don't." He pulled the jeep over at the junction, in no hurry to reach the station just yet, and sat a moment with his hands still on the wheel. "I was the first officer in that house. Twenty-six years old, my third year on the job, and I'd never seen anything like it before or since. Fifteen men, and one of them still breathing when we got there. Barely. I held his hand while the ambulance was still ten minutes out, because there was nothing else useful I could do for him, and he told me - not raving, not delirious, clear as anything - that the man who'd done it could see in the dark. Proper dark. No torch, nothing. Like it was daylight to him." He looked over at the constable for the first time since he'd started talking. "Then he died, and I never found that man, and every year since, this case has sat in my drawer, closed on paper and open in my head. I'd like to find him before I retire. Call it professional stubbornness, if you want a word for it that fits in a report."

The constable said nothing further. Somewhere across the village, Suribabu was already sending what men he had left out to search the fields and the canal banks for four more who wouldn't be found either, and Devendra, watching the road ahead rather than his own thoughts, found himself wondering - not for the first time, and not the last - whether the two searches, his and Suribabu's, might one day turn out to be looking for the very same man.
